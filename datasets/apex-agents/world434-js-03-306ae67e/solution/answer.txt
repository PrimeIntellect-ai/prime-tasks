Yes. The Tenant is entitled to terminate the lease agreement.

The lease contains an implied term warranting that the second and third floors were lawfully constructed. The lease is silent on lawful construction, but in Saha Ram Krishna v Tan Tai Joum [2024] SGHC 9 at [60]-[65], the High Court implied exactly this term in fact into a tenancy of upper storeys, applying the three-step test in Sembcorp Marine Ltd v PPL Holdings Pte Ltd [2013] 4 SLR 193. There was a gap, because the parties never addressed lawful construction. The term was necessary to give the tenancy business efficacy, because the tenant bargained for the right to use those storeys without physical or legal impediment. And the term was obvious, because the landlord as owner is better placed to know whether the building was lawfully constructed and keeps the lasting benefit of it. The same reasoning applies here: the Tenant took the lease to operate rentable bike storage on the second and third floors.

The Landlord breached that term, because the Singapore authorities informed the Tenant that the second and third floors were unlawfully constructed and cannot be used. Liability for breach of contract is strict, so it is no defence that the Landlord did not build the floors or did not know they were unlawful (Saha at [68]).

The Landlord's breach of the implied lawful-construction term was a repudiatory breach of the lease. In Saha at [69]-[73], the court held breach of that term repudiatory because it deprived the tenants of substantially the whole benefit they bargained for, even though only the third storey had been unlawfully constructed. Saha involved a separate tenancy for the upper storeys, while here a single lease covers all three floors and the first-floor bike shop remains usable. But both upper floors are unlawful, they are two of the Premises' three floors, and they house the Tenant's entire rentable storage business, which is now completely barred. That loss deprives the Tenant of substantially the whole benefit it was to receive under the lease, so the breach is repudiatory.

The Tenant has not lost its right to terminate. A party loses that right if, knowing of the breach, it affirms the contract by unequivocally continuing to perform it. In Saha at [74]-[78], the tenants lost their right to terminate because they kept paying rent for seven months after discovering the breach. Here, the Tenant sued for damages and termination immediately after the authorities told it the floors could not be used, and nothing suggests it affirmed the lease.

The Tenant is therefore entitled to terminate the lease agreement.
