Subject: Colorado Privacy Act Exposure from the BlueQuill Analytics Module

Hi [CEO],

Yes. If this incident had affected a Colorado customer, it would likely result in violations of the Colorado Privacy Act (C.R.S. § 6-1-1301 et seq.). This assumes Northstar meets the applicability thresholds in § 6-1-1304.

What happened: From May 2024 to January 14, 2025, an analytics module deployed without approval sent roughly 2.3 to 2.8 million event records from our production instance to BlueQuill Analytics, a US vendor. The confirmed payloads contained user IDs, device IDs, timestamps, event types, and QC flags. Our records describing this data flow also list customer account IDs, product-safety metadata, and health-related product identifiers.

Vendor and subprocessor practices: The module had no privacy or security review, no legal or procurement approval, and no place on any approved vendor list. There was no DPA and no SCCs with BlueQuill. That breaks our own Vendor and Subprocessor Management Policy (PRIV-01 v2), which bars any vendor from processing personal data without an intake review, an executed contract, and a register entry. We also lack a reliable process for approving subprocessors and disclosing them to customers, and we keep no central register of data transfers.

Likely violations under § 6-1-1308:

1. Northstar breached its duty of transparency under § 6-1-1308(1). The analytics processing was missing from our records of processing and vendor inventory, so our privacy notice did not disclose its purpose, the data shared, or BlueQuill as a recipient.
2. Northstar breached its duty of purpose specification under § 6-1-1308(2). We never specified third-party analytics as an express purpose for which the data is collected and processed. The activity was not recorded in our records of processing at the time.
3. Northstar breached its duty of data minimization under § 6-1-1308(3). Northstar's use of the data was beyond the scope of the consent consumers provided when we collected it. Sending it to an unapproved vendor was not adequate, relevant, and limited to what is reasonably necessary for our specified purposes.
4. Northstar breached its duty to avoid secondary use under § 6-1-1308(4). The transfer served a purpose not reasonably necessary to or compatible with our specified purposes, and consumers did not consent to that secondary use. Their prior consent did not extend to BlueQuill.
5. Northstar breached its duty of care under § 6-1-1308(5). Northstar failed to take reasonable measures to secure personal data during both storage and use from unauthorized acquisition. In use, we had no controls to monitor outbound connections, no alerts fired, and the data flow was found only while investigating an outage. In storage, the data sat on BlueQuill's US servers under its own 30-day log retention, outside our controls and without any contract.
6. Northstar may have breached its duty regarding sensitive data under § 6-1-1308(7). Northstar may not have obtained the requisite consent to process consumers' sensitive data, including data handled by its vendors. Our policy requires every vendor to be bound by a written agreement meeting GDPR Article 28 requirements before it handles personal data, yet BlueQuill confirmed no SCCs were in place, our own review found no DPA or transfer documentation, and the gap assessment found no adequate privacy safeguards over vendor onboarding. Our records also list health-related product identifiers in this data flow.
