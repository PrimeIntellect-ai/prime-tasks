[Transcription begins]

Q: Dr. Martinez, thanks for speaking with me. Can you describe what you're seeing in terms of childhood lead exposure in your practice here in Hartford?

A: Sure. So I've been practicing pediatric medicine in Hartford for about twelve years now, and lead exposure remains one of our most persistent public health challenges. We screen every child at ages one and two, and then again if they're in a high-risk area. In the last couple of years, we've been seeing a slight decrease in the most severe cases -- the ones above 10 micrograms per deciliter -- but the moderate cases, between 3.5 and 5, those are not going down as fast as we'd like.

Q: The CDC lowered its reference value to 3.5 micrograms per deciliter. Has that affected how you manage cases?

A: Absolutely. When Connecticut adopted the 3.5 threshold in January 2023 under Public Act 22-49, it meant we were identifying more children earlier, which is good from a prevention standpoint. But it also put more pressure on local health departments for case management. Our clinic alone saw about a 30 percent increase in referrals for lead case management after the threshold change. The demand for services outpaced the resources.

Q: What are the primary sources of lead exposure you see in your patients?

A: Overwhelmingly, it's deteriorating lead paint in older housing. Hartford has a lot of pre-1978 housing stock. But I'm also increasingly concerned about drinking water. Many families in my practice use unfiltered tap water. When I hear about the EPA's new rules to replace lead service lines within ten years, I think that's a step in the right direction, but ten years is a long time when you're talking about a child's developing brain.

Q: Can you walk me through what happens when a child tests above 3.5?

A: We do a confirmatory venous blood draw first, because the initial finger-prick screening can sometimes give false positives. If the confirmatory test is also elevated, we report it to the local health department. They're supposed to conduct an environmental investigation within a certain timeframe -- looking at the home, the water, the soil. And we start more frequent monitoring of the child's blood lead levels.

Q: Are there demographic disparities you've observed?

A: Without question. The majority of my patients who test elevated are children of color, particularly Black and Hispanic children. This tracks with the state data. It's a function of housing -- families who can least afford it are often living in the oldest, most poorly maintained housing. And it's a function of historic disinvestment in certain neighborhoods. Lead poisoning is fundamentally an environmental justice issue.

Q: Some have argued that the new EPA Lead and Copper Rule Improvements will help address the water-side of this. What's your take?

A: I think the intent is good. Lowering the action level from 15 to 10 parts per billion and requiring full replacement of lead service lines -- those are meaningful policy changes. But implementation is everything. We've seen in places like Newark and Flint that even when there are rules on the books, enforcement can be inconsistent. I'd like to see more funding for local health departments to do the testing and follow-up work.

Q: Is there anything else you'd want the public to know?

A: Lead poisoning is entirely preventable. That's the thing that makes it so frustrating. We know what causes it, we know how to fix it, and yet hundreds of thousands of children are still being exposed every year. Every child deserves clean water and safe housing. That shouldn't be controversial.

[Transcription ends]
