You are a Social Science Research Assistant at a university research center. Your supervisor is preparing a comparative manuscript on global climate change attitudes and needs you to harmonize, validate, and integrate data from multiple heterogeneous sources. A reviewer has specifically requested a unified cross-study comparison with standardized metrics — simply summarizing each source separately will not suffice.

Your working folder contains "climate_policy_survey_raw.db", a SQLite database of raw survey responses from approximately 800 respondents across 15 countries, and "Climate_Change_Opinion_Survey_2023_aggregated.csv", the Meta/Yale Climate Change Opinion Survey 2023 aggregated dataset with country-level proportions. You will also need to consult two external sources: the Meta/Yale Climate Change Opinion Survey codebook covering 2021 to 2023, published by the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication and Data for Good at Meta on the Humanitarian Data Exchange (data.humdata.org), and the ESS Topline Results Series issue 9, "European Attitudes to Climate Change and Energy: Topline Results from Round 8 of the European Social Survey" (Poortinga et al., 2018), which reported findings from 23 European countries based on 2016–2017 fieldwork.

Your supervisor writes: "We need a single integrated dataset and analysis workbook that lets the reviewer directly compare our survey findings against Meta/Yale and ESS benchmarks on a country-by-country basis. Please standardize the variables across sources so they are comparable, flag where constructs don't align perfectly, and note any countries where our results diverge significantly from established surveys. The regression models should control for the country-level macro indicators we have. Also, the IRB office wants the cleaned database back in SQLite format, not just CSV."

Produce three deliverables. First, "harmonized_survey.db" — a cleaned SQLite database containing a "cleaned_responses" table with a "data_quality_flag" column documenting all corrections applied, a "variable_crosswalk" table mapping each of our survey variables to the closest Meta/Yale and ESS constructs with comparability assessments, and a "country_metadata" table retaining the original macro indicators. Second, "cross_study_analysis.xlsx" — a multi-sheet Excel workbook with sheets "Country_Benchmarks" (our survey percentages alongside Meta/Yale and ESS benchmarks per country, with differences computed and non-overlapping countries marked N/A), "Descriptive_Stats" (mean, SD, median by country and overall for Likert-scale variables), "Correlation_Matrix" (Pearson correlations among Likert-scale variables), "Regression_Models" (two OLS regressions: (a) carbon_tax_support predicted by cc_belief, cc_worry, political_ideology, education, trust_scientists, gdp_per_capita, and climate_vulnerability_index; (b) personal_action_willingness predicted by cc_worry, trust_government, income_level, age, and co2_per_capita — report coefficients, standard errors, p-values, and R-squared), and "Divergence_Flags" (countries deviating more than 15 percentage points from benchmarks with explanations). Third, "reconciliation_memo.docx" — a methodological memo analyzing variable mapping imperfections across sources, alignment patterns for countries appearing in all three datasets, and specific recommendations for qualifying cross-study comparisons in the manuscript.
